# The MVP: The Smallest Thing That Teaches You Something

You have a great idea. You can already picture every feature. The temptation is to build all of it and unveil it in a big, glorious launch.

Please don't. That's how people spend a year building something nobody wants.

## What's an MVP?

**MVP** stands for **Minimum Viable Product.** It's the smallest version of your idea that lets real people do the one core thing, so you can learn if it's worth building more.

It's not a bad version. It's a **small, focused** version.

## A Classic Example

Want to start a food delivery service? You don't build an app, hire drivers, and sign restaurants. You post a menu on a simple page, take orders by text, and deliver them yourself. If people keep ordering, then you build more.

That's an MVP. It's a little embarrassing, and it teaches you a lot.

## How to Decide What's In

Use your **problem statement** as the tiebreaker. For a plant-reminder app, the problem is "first-time plant owners forget to water." So:

- **In the MVP:** add a plant, get a reminder, mark it watered. They directly solve the problem.
- **Later:** a disease-scanning camera, a social feed, a plant shop. They're different problems or need big new systems.

Ask of every feature: **"Does one user need this to get the core value?"** If not, it waits.

## The Test: Can You Fake It?

Before you build anything, ask: could I do this by hand for my first 20 customers? If yes, do it by hand. Packing coffee boxes yourself is an MVP. Building a warehouse system is not.

## Common MVP Mistakes

- **Too big:** "Minimum" means minimum. Cut more than feels comfortable.
- **Too broken:** "Viable" means it must actually work for the core job.
- **No question:** Decide what you want to learn before you build. ("Will people come back after a week?")
- **Never measuring:** An MVP you don't learn from is just a small product.
