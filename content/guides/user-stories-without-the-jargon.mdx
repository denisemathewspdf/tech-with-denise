# User Stories Without the Jargon

Compare these two:

- **"Build a notification system."**
- **"As a first-time plant owner, I want a reminder on the days my plant needs water, so that I stop letting it dry out."**

The first one tells an engineer what to build. The second tells them **who it's for and why it matters.** That's a user story.

## The Formula

> **As a** [type of person], **I want** [something they want to do], **so that** [the reason it matters].

Three parts, one sentence. That's it.

## Why Bother?

Because "what" without "why" leads to the wrong solution. If an engineer only knows "build a notification system," they might build something fancy that no one needs. If they know the user forgets to water plants, they can suggest a simpler, better idea.

A story keeps everyone, from the PM to the designer to the engineer, aimed at the same real human.

## Writing a Good One

- **Name a specific person.** "As a first-time plant owner" beats "As a user."
- **Describe what they want to do, not how.** "I want a reminder" is better than "I want a push notification at 8am."
- **Make the "so that" real.** It should be the actual benefit, not a restatement. ("So that I can see reminders" is useless.)
- **Keep it small.** If a story takes a month, split it.

## Add "Done" Criteria

A story is better with a short checklist of what must be true when it's finished. These are called **acceptance criteria**:

- A reminder appears on the day the plant needs water.
- Tapping the reminder opens that plant.
- Marking it watered clears the reminder.

Now everyone agrees what "done" means.

## Common Mistakes

- **Writing tasks instead of stories.** "Create a database table" is a task, not a story.
- **Making the user "the user."** Be specific about who they are.
- **Skipping the why.** If you can't write the "so that," you might not understand the problem yet.
