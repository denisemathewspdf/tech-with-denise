# Gantt Charts Without the Panic

You've seen it before. A wall of colorful horizontal bars, tiny arrows, and a lot of people nodding like they understand it. It looks intense.

Take a breath. A Gantt chart is **a calendar with opinions.** That's all.

## How to Read One

Picture a table:

- **Down the left side**: your tasks, in order.
- **Across the top**: time (days, weeks, or months).
- **Each bar**: when a task starts and when it ends. A longer bar means it takes longer.

Read it left to right like a sentence: "Sketching happens in weeks 1 and 2. Then inking happens in weeks 3 to 10. Then we format the book in week 11."

(It's named after Henry Gantt, an engineer who helped make these popular in the early 1900s. Fun fact: he did this before computers, so he drew them by hand.)

## The Part That Makes It Powerful: Dependencies

Some tasks can't start until another one finishes. You can't approve a proof copy you haven't ordered. That's a **dependency**, and on a Gantt chart it shows up as a bar that starts only after another one ends.

This is the whole point of a Gantt chart. It stops you from scheduling things in an order that can't work.

## How to Make Your First One

You don't need special software. Try this:

1. **List your tasks.** Break the project into small chunks.
2. **Estimate how long each takes.** (If drawing 30 illustrations takes 2 hours each and you have 6 hours a week, that's 10 weeks.)
3. **Find the dependencies.** Which tasks must wait for which?
4. **Place the tasks on the calendar.** Nothing starts before the thing it depends on.
5. **Add a buffer.** Leave an extra week or two before the deadline, because stuff always takes longer.

Draw it on paper, in a spreadsheet, or in a free tool. The clarity comes from thinking it through, not from fancy software.

## Common Mistakes (So You Can Skip Them)

- **Forgetting the buffer.** A plan that finishes exactly on deadline day has no room for real life.
- **Making tasks too big.** "Build the app" is not a task. "Build the login screen" is.
- **Making it once and never looking at it again.** A Gantt chart is a living plan. Update it when reality changes.
- **Hiding it.** The whole point is that everyone can see it.

<Callout variant="tip">
If a Gantt chart ever feels overwhelming, zoom out. Look at the big phases first (Define, Plan, Execute, Close), then zoom in on one phase at a time.
</Callout>

## When You Don't Need One

Tiny project? A simple checklist is probably enough. Gantt charts shine when you have lots of tasks that depend on each other and a deadline you care about.
