# The Daily Standup Without the Dread

Every morning, the team gathers for 15 minutes, and somehow it becomes 40. Someone tells a long story. Someone else solves a bug out loud while five people stare at their shoes.

A good standup is the opposite. It's short, useful, and a little boring, in the best way.

## What It's For

The **daily scrum** (or standup) has one job: help the team **sync up and spot trouble early.** It's not a status report for the boss. It's for the team to plan the next 24 hours together.

## The Classic Three Questions

Each person shares:

1. **What did I do yesterday** toward our sprint goal?
2. **What will I do today?**
3. **What's in my way?** (Blockers)

That's it.

## A Good Update vs a Bad One

**Bad:** "I'm working on stuff."

**Also bad:** "Fine." (Or 12 minutes about one bug.)

**Good:** "Yesterday I finished the menu page. Today I'll start on toppings. I'm blocked on the photos from the shop."

See the difference? You know what happened, what's next, and what help is needed.

## Rules That Keep It Healthy

- **Same time, same place, every day.** Predictable beats perfect.
- **15 minutes, max.** Stand up if it helps you stay short.
- **Take long discussions offline.** Say "let's talk after," and then actually do.
- **Name blockers out loud.** The Scrum Master's job is to help clear them.
- **Talk to each other.** Don't just report to the manager.

## For Remote Teams

Use a short written standup in chat, or keep video calls to 15 minutes with cameras optional. The key is the rhythm, not the format.

## When to Change It

If your standup feels pointless, ask the team what's missing. Maybe it should focus on the sprint board instead of each person. Maybe two people should talk after. Standups are allowed to evolve.

<Callout variant="tip">
The best standup question is the one nobody wants to ask: "What are we worried about?" It surfaces risks before they become fires.
</Callout>
